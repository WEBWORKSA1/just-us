A good first date has one job: let two people find out whether they enjoy each other's company. Everything else, including the venue, the outfit and the bill, either helps that or gets in the way.

Three tests for any first date plan

  • Can you talk? A loud bar or a film gives you nothing to go on.
  • Is there something to look at? A shared focus fills silences naturally and gives you things to react to.
  • Can it end after an hour or stretch to three? A short default with an easy extension suits both outcomes.

Settings that pass all three

  • A walk with a drink in hand. Side by side is less intense than face to face, and the scenery keeps changing.
  • A market, bookshop or museum. Every stall, shelf and painting is a conversation starter.
  • Something mildly silly. Mini golf, bowling or an arcade. It is hard to stay nervous while losing at air hockey.
  • Coffee or dessert rather than dinner. Cheaper, shorter and easier to extend if it is going well.

Questions worth bringing

Skip the interview questions about jobs and siblings; they produce facts, not conversation. Ask things that invite a story: the best trip you have taken, what you were like at ten, what you are looking forward to. Then ask a follow-up before offering your own answer. There are twenty-five easy openers in the getting-to-know-you deck.

Practical points

  • Meet in public and make your own way there and back. Tell a friend where you will be.
  • Suggest a specific plan. “Coffee at the market on Saturday at eleven?” is far easier to say yes to than “we should hang out”.
  • Put the phone away. Attention is the most attractive thing you can bring.
  • Say so if you enjoyed it. A plain “I had a good time, I would like to do this again” saves two people a week of guessing.
