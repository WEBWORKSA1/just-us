How to ask better questions (and actually hear the answers)

By the Just.us editors · Updated November 20, 2026 · 4 min read

A list of questions is only paper. What makes a conversation feel close is how the questions are asked and, even more, what happens after the answer. In a well-known 1997 study, psychologist Arthur Aron and colleagues paired strangers and had them work through questions that became gradually more personal. Those pairs reported feeling closer than pairs who made small talk. The questions mattered less than the pattern: both people sharing, a little more each time, and being listened to.

1. Start shallow, go deeper slowly

Opening with “what is your biggest regret?” over a Tuesday dinner rarely works. Warm up with something light from the fun deck, then move toward the deep one. Depth is earned by the previous answer going well.

2. Ask one question, then stop talking

Resist adding a second question or your own answer. Leave a pause. People often give their real answer after the first, easier one, and only if there is room.

3. Follow up before you take your turn

The most generous words in a conversation are “tell me more about that”. One follow-up shows you were listening and usually produces the best part of the story. Then answer the same question yourself. Sharing has to go both ways.

4. Listen to understand, not to fix

When your partner describes a worry, the instinct is to solve it. Try asking first: “do you want ideas, or do you want me to just listen?” Most of the time it is the second.

5. Let answers be different from yours

The point of asking is to find out something you did not know. If an answer surprises or even unsettles you, be curious before you respond. “I did not know that. When did that start?” keeps the door open; a quick rebuttal closes it.

6. Know when to stop

Three or four good questions make an evening. Twenty make an interrogation. End while it is still enjoyable and you will both want to do it again.
